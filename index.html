Case Math Basics

01

How to do math out loud

Case math is rarely hard. Interviewers are grading whether you set it up clearly, keep track of units, and say what the answer means for the client.

1. Say the formula first

"Break-even units equals fixed costs divided by the profit on each unit." Then plug in numbers. If you slip on the arithmetic, the interviewer can still see you know the method.

2. Write units on every line

Dollars or millions, per month or per year, per customer or in total. Most case math mistakes are unit mistakes, not arithmetic.

3. Round on purpose, and say so

"I'll call 48 about 50 to keep it clean." Interviewers expect rounding. They just want to hear it.

4. One step at a time

Multiply two numbers, write the result, then the next. Long chains in your head are where zeros go missing.

5. Sanity-check

Does the answer make sense against something you know? 560 coffees a day is one a minute for ten hours. Is that realistic?

6. Say the so-what

Never stop at a number. "That's 3 percent of the market, which looks achievable." The so-what is what gets you credit.

If you need a moment, ask for it: "Can I take 30 seconds to set this up?" That's normal and looks better than rushing.

02

Big numbers and mental math

Cases are full of millions and billions. The trick is to separate the front numbers from the zeros.

Count the zeros

Thousand has 3 zeros (K), million 6 (M), billion 9 (B). To multiply, multiply the front numbers and add the zeros. To divide, divide the front numbers and subtract the zeros.

Friendly multipliers

Times 5 is times 10, then half. Times 25 is times 100, then divide by 4. Divide by 5 is times 2, then divide by 10.

Break it apart

37 times 8 is 30 times 8, plus 7 times 8. That's 240 plus 56, or 296.

Round, then adjust

48 times 21 is about 50 times 20, or 1,000. The exact answer is 1,008, close enough for a case.

3M × 40K → 3 × 40 = 120, zeros 6 + 3 = 9 → 120B

Interviewer

A country has 330 million people. 4 percent buy our product each year, spending 250 dollars each. How big is the market?

Your move

Find 1 percent first, then scale. Turn times 250 into something friendlier.

  1. 1 · Find 1%1% of 330M3.3M
  2. 2 · Scale to 4%3.3M × 413.2M
  3. 3 · Times $250× 1,000 = $13.2B, then ÷ 4$3.3B
  4. 4 · Sanity check$3.3B ÷ 330M people = $10 each a year✓

Common traps

  • Losing a zero. Write the unit, like M or B, on every line.
  • Mixing per month and per year. Convert everything to one before you multiply.
  • Saying "13.2 million" when you mean billion. Read your answer back once.

Quick check: 2.5 million times 400 is…

2.5 times 4 is 10. Zeros: 6 from million plus 2 from 400 is 8. 10 with 8 more zeros is 1,000,000,000, or 1 billion.

1. 45 thousand times 3,000?

"135 million. 45 times 3 is 135, and 3 plus 3 is 6 zeros."

2. 7.2 billion divided by 900?

"8 million. Think of it as 7,200 million divided by 900, which is 8 million."

3. 16 times 25?

"400. 16 times 100 is 1,600, divided by 4."

4. 18 percent of 450?

"81. 10 percent is 45, 5 percent is 22.5, 3 percent is 13.5. 45 plus 22.5 plus 13.5 is 81."

03

Percentages

The most common case math, and the easiest to trip on. Almost every mistake comes from dividing by the wrong number.

Percent of

X percent of Y is X divided by 100, times Y. Flip trick: 8 percent of 50 is the same as 50 percent of 8, which is 4.

Percent change

New minus old, divided by old. Always divide by the starting number.

Points vs. percent

A margin going from 20 to 25 percent is up 5 percentage points, but that's a 25 percent increase. Say which one you mean.

Going backward

If 120 dollars is the price after a 20 percent increase, the original is 120 divided by 1.2, or 100. Not 120 minus 20 percent.

Changes stack, they don't add

Up 10 percent, then down 10 percent, is 1.1 times 0.9, or 0.99. That's a 1 percent drop, not zero.

Share

Part divided by whole. 30 out of 200 customers is 15 percent. Market share works the same way.

% change = (new − old) ÷ old

Interviewer

Revenue grew from 80 million to 100 million, and the profit margin went from 10 percent to 12 percent. How much did profit grow?

Your move

Work out profit before and after, then the percent change. Don't just add the two growth rates.

  1. 1 · Revenue growth(100 − 80) ÷ 8025%
  2. 2 · Old profit$80M × 10%$8M
  3. 3 · New profit$100M × 12%$12M
  4. 4 · Profit growth(12 − 8) ÷ 850%
  5. 5 · Margin change10% → 12%: +2 points, or +20%+2 pts
  6. 6 · So whatProfit grew 2× as fast as revenue✓

Common traps

  • Dividing by the new number instead of the old one.
  • Undoing a percent by subtracting the same percent. A 20 percent drop needs a 25 percent rise to recover.
  • Saying "percent" when you mean "percentage points."

Quick check: a price rises 25 percent to 50 dollars. What was it before?

50 divided by 1.25 is 40. Check: 40 plus 25 percent of 40, which is 10, is 50. Subtracting 25 percent of 50 gives 37.50, which is the trap.

1. 15 percent of 240?

"36. 10 percent is 24, and 5 percent is half that, 12."

2. Sales fell from 250 to 200. What's the percent change, and what increase gets back to 250?

"Down 20 percent, because 50 is a fifth of 250. Getting back needs up 25 percent, because 50 is a quarter of 200."

3. Market share goes from 8 to 10 percent. How much is that?

"Up 2 percentage points, which is a 25 percent increase in share."

4. A stock falls 50 percent, then rises 50 percent. Where does it end up?

"Down 25 percent. 0.5 times 1.5 is 0.75. 100 dollars falls to 50, then rises 25 to 75."

04

Growth and compounding

Growth builds on itself. Each year grows from the year before, not from the starting point.

Compounding

Value after some years equals start times one plus the growth rate, once for each year. 10 percent for two years is 1.1 times 1.1, or 1.21, up 21 percent.

Rule of 72

Years to double is about 72 divided by the growth rate. At 8 percent a year, something doubles in about 9 years.

Average yearly growth (CAGR)

The steady yearly rate that gets you from start to end. In an interview, estimate it: guess a rate, compound it, and adjust.

Don't just divide

Going from 100 to 150 over 5 years isn't 10 percent a year. It's about 8.4 percent, because each year's growth builds on the last.

Future value = start × (1 + growth)^years · Years to double ≈ 72 ÷ growth %

Interviewer

A 200 million dollar market grows 10 percent a year. How big will it be in three years?

Your move

Grow it one year at a time. Then sanity-check with the rule of 72.

  1. Year 1$200M × 1.1$220M
  2. Year 2$220M × 1.1$242M
  3. Year 3$242M × 1.1≈$266M
  4. CompareSimple: 200 + 3 × 20 = 260. Compounding adds ≈$6M+$6M
  5. Rule of 72 check72 ÷ 10 ≈ 7 years to double; +33% in 3 years fits✓

Common traps

  • Adding yearly growth rates instead of compounding them.
  • Dividing total growth by years to get the yearly rate. It overstates it.
  • Forgetting a drop needs a bigger rise to recover.

Quick check: revenue doubles in 6 years. About what yearly growth is that?

Rule of 72: 72 divided by 6 is 12 percent. Simply dividing 100 percent by 6 years gives about 17, which overstates it.

1. 50 million growing 20 percent a year for 2 years?

"72 million. 50 times 1.2 is 60, and 60 times 1.2 is 72."

2. Costs grow 6 percent a year. About how long until they double?

"About 12 years. 72 divided by 6."

3. Sales went from 100 to 121 in 2 years. What's the yearly growth?

"10 percent a year. 1.1 times 1.1 is 1.21."

4. A market grows 3 percent a year for 10 years. Roughly how much bigger is it?

"About a third bigger. It would take 24 years to double, so 10 years gets less than halfway. The exact answer is about 34 percent."

05

Profit, costs, and margins

The profit tree is the backbone of most cases. Profit is revenue minus costs, and each side breaks down further.

Revenue

Price times quantity. Or number of customers times what each spends.

Fixed vs. variable costs

Fixed costs don't change with volume, like rent and salaried staff. Variable costs rise with each unit, like ingredients and packaging.

Margin

Profit divided by revenue. A 15 percent margin means 15 cents of profit on every dollar of sales.

Contribution per unit

Price minus variable cost per unit. What each sale adds toward covering fixed costs.

Margin vs. markup

Cost 60, price 100. Margin is 40 divided by the price, 40 percent. Markup is 40 divided by the cost, about 67 percent.

EBITDA

Profit from operations, before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. The profit number deals are priced on.

Profit = price × units − (fixed costs + variable cost × units)

Interviewer

A coffee shop sells 400 cups a day, 360 days a year, at 4 dollars a cup. Each cup costs 1.50 dollars to make, and fixed costs are 250 thousand dollars a year. What's the profit and margin?

Your move

Get yearly volume first. Then revenue, variable costs, and fixed costs, in that order.

  1. 1 · Cups a year400 × 360144,000
  2. 2 · Revenue144,000 × $4$576K
  3. 3 · Variable costs144,000 × $1.50$216K
  4. 4 · Contribution$576K − $216K ($2.50 a cup)$360K
  5. 5 · Profit$360K − $250K fixed$110K
  6. 6 · Margin$110K ÷ $576K≈19%

Common traps

  • Mixing up margin and markup. Margin divides by price; markup divides by cost.
  • Scaling fixed costs with volume. They stay put.
  • Mixing per-unit and total numbers in the same line.

Quick check: a product costs 30 dollars and sells for 50. What's the margin?

Profit is 20. 20 divided by the 50 dollar price is 40 percent. 20 divided by the 30 dollar cost is the markup, about 67 percent.

1. Revenue is 80 million at a 15 percent margin. What's the profit?

"12 million. 10 percent is 8, 5 percent is 4."

2. Price 25 dollars, variable cost 10, sell 4,000 units, fixed costs 40 thousand. Profit?

"20 thousand. Each unit contributes 15 dollars. 15 times 4,000 is 60 thousand, minus 40 thousand fixed."

3. A product costs 75 dollars. What price gives a 25 percent margin?

"100 dollars. Cost must be 75 percent of price, so 75 divided by 0.75. Adding 25 percent to the cost gives 93.75, which is a markup, not a margin."

4. Revenue and every cost both rise 10 percent. What happens to profit and margin?

"Profit rises 10 percent too, but the margin stays the same, because profit and revenue grew at the same rate."

06

Break-even and payback

Break-even asks how much you need to sell to cover your costs. Payback asks how long it takes to earn back an up-front investment.

Break-even units

Fixed costs divided by contribution per unit. Each sale chips in its contribution until fixed costs are covered.

With a profit target

Fixed costs plus the target profit, divided by contribution per unit.

Break-even price

For a set volume: variable cost per unit, plus fixed costs divided by units.

Payback period

Up-front cost divided by yearly profit, or yearly savings. A 500 thousand dollar machine that saves 125 thousand a year pays back in 4 years.

Reality check 1: per day

Turn yearly break-even into a daily number. 200 thousand cups a year is about 550 a day. Can one shop do that?

Reality check 2: share of market

Divide break-even by the market size. Needing 3 percent sounds doable; needing 40 percent doesn't.

Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per unit)

Interviewer

A new gym charges 50 dollars a month. Each member costs 10 dollars a month to serve, and fixed costs are 60 thousand a month. The town has 40 thousand adults. How many members to break even, and how long to pay back a 1.2 million dollar build if it reaches 2,000 members?

Your move

Say the formula first. Keep everything monthly until the last step.

  1. 1 · Contribution$50 − $10 a member a month$40
  2. 2 · Break-even members$60,000 ÷ $401,500
  3. 3 · Share of adults1,500 ÷ 40,0003.75%
  4. 4 · Profit at 2,000 members2,000 × $40 − $60,000$20K/mo
  5. 5 · Payback$1.2M ÷ $20K a month = 60 months5 years
  6. 6 · So whatReachable break-even, slow payback✓

Common traps

  • Dividing fixed costs by the price instead of the contribution.
  • Mixing monthly and yearly numbers.
  • Treating a one-time build cost as a yearly fixed cost. Use payback for one-time costs.

Quick check: fixed costs are 100 thousand, price is 20 dollars, and variable cost is 15. How many units to break even?

Each unit contributes 5 dollars. 100 thousand divided by 5 is 20 thousand. Dividing by the 20 dollar price gives 5,000, which ignores variable costs.

1. Price 8 dollars, variable cost 3, fixed costs 50 thousand. How many units for a 25 thousand dollar profit?

"15 thousand. 50 plus 25 is 75 thousand to cover, divided by 5 dollars a unit."

2. A 500 thousand dollar machine saves 125 thousand a year. Payback?

"4 years. 500 divided by 125."

3. A 200-room hotel charges 150 dollars, with 50 dollars of cost per occupied night and 6 million of fixed costs a year. How many nights must rooms be filled?

"300 nights a year. A full night brings in 200 rooms times 100 dollars, or 20 thousand. 6 million divided by 20 thousand is 300. That's about 82 percent occupancy, which is risky."

4. Same as the quick check, but the price drops from 20 to 18 dollars. What happens to break-even?

"It jumps from 20 thousand to about 33 thousand units, up two-thirds. A 10 percent price cut shrinks the contribution from 5 dollars to 3."

Probability and expected value

Used for risks, launches, and anything uncertain. The idea: weight each outcome by its chance.

Expected value

Value times chance. With several outcomes, add up each value times its chance.

Expected yearly loss

Cost if it happens, times the chance per year. A 20 percent chance a year is about once every five years, so spread the cost over five years.

All must happen: multiply

Pass step 1 at 50 percent and step 2 at 60 percent: 0.5 times 0.6 is 30 percent for both.

Doesn't happen

One minus the chance. A 20 percent chance of rain is an 80 percent chance of none.

At least once

One minus the chance it never happens. 20 percent a year for two years: 1 minus 0.8 times 0.8, or 36 percent.

Break-even chance

The chance of success at which the expected value is exactly zero. Above it, go; below it, don't.

Expected value = Σ (outcome × chance) · All steps: multiply the chances

Interviewer

A product launch has a 60 percent chance of success, earning 50 million. If it fails, the company loses 20 million. Should it launch?

Your move

Weight each outcome by its chance and add them. Then find the break-even chance.

  1. 1 · Success60% × $50M$30M
  2. 2 · Failure40% × −$20M−$8M
  3. 3 · Expected value$30M − $8M+$22M
  4. 4 · Break-even chanceDownside ÷ total swing: $20M ÷ ($50M + $20M)≈29%
  5. 5 · So what60% is well above the 29% break-even✓

Common traps

  • Adding chances that all have to happen. Multiply them.
  • Confusing the impact with the expected value. 45 million if it happens, at 20 percent, is 9 million expected.
  • Forgetting the failure case, which is often negative.

Quick check: a drug must pass three stages at 50, 60, and 50 percent. What's the chance it passes all three?

0.5 times 0.6 is 0.3, times 0.5 is 0.15. Averaging gives about 53, and adding gives 160, both wrong, because each stage only applies to the ones that passed the stage before.

1. A 30 million dollar lawsuit has a 10 percent chance a year. Expected yearly cost?

"3 million a year. About once every 10 years, so 30 divided by 10."

2. 80 percent times 30 percent times 50 percent times 75 percent?

"9 percent. 80 times 30 is 24, times 50 is 12, times 75 is 9."

3. A bid costs half a million to prepare and has a 25 percent chance of winning a contract worth 4 million in profit. Bid?

"Yes. 25 percent of 4 million is 1 million, minus the half million cost, is plus half a million expected."

4. A risk has a 20 percent chance each year. What's the chance it happens at least once in 2 years?

"36 percent. The chance it never happens is 0.8 times 0.8, or 64 percent. 100 minus 64 is 36."

08

Valuation and deal math

Deal cases price a company from its yearly profit. One rule does most of the work: price equals yearly EBITDA times a multiple.

Price from profit

Value equals yearly EBITDA times a multiple. The multiple is how many years of today's profit a buyer pays up front.

Where the multiple comes from

What similar companies sold for, divided by their EBITDA. Faster growth and lower risk push it up. In interviews, ask for it; don't invent it.

Repeats every year: multiply

Anything that changes yearly profit changes the price by that amount times the multiple. Losing 2 million a year at 12 times is 24 million of value.

One-time: dollar for dollar

A one-time fine, debt, or cleanup cost comes off the price once. A 5 million fine is 5 million less, not 60.

Business vs. shares

The multiple prices the whole business. Subtract debt and add cash to get what the shares are worth.

Premium and synergies

A premium is what a buyer pays above the standalone value. It's worth it only if synergies, times a multiple, minus one-time costs, are worth more.

Business value = EBITDA × multiple · Share value = business value − debt + cash

Interviewer

A seller asks 200 million for a company with 10 million of EBITDA. Diligence finds 1.5 million of that came from a one-time sale. The company has 30 million of debt and 5 million of cash. What are the shares worth at the same multiple?

Your move

Find the multiple the seller is using. Fix the profit, value the business, then move from the business to the shares.

  1. 1 · Seller's multiple$200M ÷ $10M20×
  2. 2 · Real EBITDA$10M − $1.5M one-time$8.5M
  3. 3 · Business value$8.5M × 20$170M
  4. 4 · Net debt$30M debt − $5M cash$25M
  5. 5 · Share value$170M − $25M$145M
  6. 6 · So what−$30M for the profit fix, −$25M for net debt✓

Common traps

  • Multiplying a one-time item. Only yearly changes get the multiple.
  • Forgetting debt when the question asks what the shares are worth.
  • Mixing an EBITDA multiple with net income, or the other way around.

Quick check: a company has 12 million of EBITDA, a 9 times multiple, and 20 million of net debt. What are the shares worth?

12 times 9 is 108 million for the business. Subtract 20 million of net debt: 88 million for the shares.

1. A company valued at 12 times has a 5 million one-time legal settlement. How much does the price fall?

"5 million. It's one-time, so it comes off dollar for dollar."

2. The same company loses a customer worth 2 million of EBITDA a year. How much does the price fall?

"24 million. It repeats every year, so 2 million times 12."

3. A buyer pays a 30 million premium. Synergies are 6 million a year, valued at 8 times, with 10 million of one-time integration costs. Worth it?

"Yes, by about 8 million. 6 times 8 is 48, minus 10 is 38 million of synergy value, against a 30 million premium."

4. What's 1 million a year forever worth, if investors want an 8 percent return?

"12.5 million. 1 million divided by 8 percent. That's a 12.5 times multiple, which is where multiples come from: the return investors want, flipped."

09

Market sizing

No one expects the exact number. Interviewers grade a clear structure, sensible assumptions said out loud, clean math, and a sanity check.

Top-down

Start big and narrow: population, times the share who buy, times how often, times the price.

Bottom-up

Start with one unit and scale: one store's sales, times the number of stores.

People or households?

Use households for things bought per home, like internet or sofas. Use people for personal items, like toothbrushes.

Segment when groups differ

Split kids and adults, or homes and businesses, when they buy very differently.

Market = units × share who buy × how often × price

Interviewer

Estimate the yearly market for dog food in a city of 2 million people.

Your move

Go through households, since dogs live in homes. State every assumption. Then check it a second way.

  1. 1 · Households2M people ÷ 2.5 per home800K
  2. 2 · Dogs800K × 30%, one dog each240K
  3. 3 · Bags a year240K × 122.88M
  4. 4 · Market2.88M × $40≈$115M
  5. 5 · Bottom-up check$115M ÷ 100 stores ≈ $3K a day each✓

Every number here is an interview-style assumption, not market data.

Common traps

  • Not saying assumptions out loud, so the interviewer can't follow or correct you.
  • Mixing people and households in the same chain.
  • Skipping the sanity check, which is where wrong zeros get caught.

Quick check: which of these is bottom-up?

Bottom-up starts from a single unit, one shop, and scales up. The others start from a big total and narrow it, which is top-down.

1. 500 stores each sell 2 million dollars a year. Market?

"1 billion dollars. 500 times 2 million."

2. A city of 1 million. 40 percent drive to work, 2 trips a day, 250 workdays. How many car commute trips a year?

"200 million. 400 thousand drivers, times 2, is 800 thousand trips a day, times 250 days."

3. A 500 million dollar market. You have 8 percent. What share do you need for 60 million in sales?

"12 percent. You're at 40 million today. 60 divided by 500 is 12 percent, up 4 points."

Weighted averages and mix

When groups are different sizes, a simple average is wrong. Weight each group by how big it is.

Weighted average

Multiply each value by its share, then add. Shares must add to 100 percent.

Mix shift

The overall average can change even when no single part changes, just because the mix moved toward one group.

Weighted average = Σ (value × share)

Interviewer

Product A is 70 percent of sales at a 20 percent margin. Product B is 30 percent of sales at a 50 percent margin. What's the overall margin, and what happens if the mix moves to 50-50?

Your move

Weight each margin by its share of sales, then add.

  1. 1 · Product A70% × 20%14 pts
  2. 2 · Product B30% × 50%15 pts
  3. 3 · Overall margin14 + 15 (not the simple average of 35)29%
  4. 4 · At 50-5050% × 20% + 50% × 50%35%
  5. 5 · So whatMix alone adds 6 points of margin+6 pts

Quick check: 20 students average 80 on a test, and 30 students average 90. What's the class average?

20 times 80 is 1,600. 30 times 90 is 2,700. Together, 4,300 points over 50 students is 86. The simple average of 85 ignores that the second group is bigger.

1. 100 rooms sell for 200 dollars and 300 rooms for 100. Average room rate?

"125 dollars. 20 thousand plus 30 thousand is 50 thousand, over 400 rooms."

2. Region A is 60 percent of revenue and grows 5 percent. Region B is 40 percent and grows 20 percent. Total growth?

"11 percent. 60 percent of 5 is 3, plus 40 percent of 20 is 8."

3. 80 percent of customers pay 10 dollars and 20 percent pay 50. Average?

"18 dollars. 8 plus 10."
